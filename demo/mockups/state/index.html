Pulse: how we read a news story, step by step

The goal is to read a news article on an ordinary computer and answer five questions about it, fast and honestly. This page tells how far we got, in order. Each chapter makes one point.

The goal: five answers from every article, on a normal CPU

For each news article we want five answers: what kind of story it is, what topic, what changed and in which direction, how big it is, and a few traits. The answers must come quickly from a computer without a graphics card. A small model, called the BitMob Reader, was built to do this.

One more rule: an answer only counts if we can show evidence that it is right. Claude agreeing with it does not count as evidence.

We found the reader was only reading the start of each story

The BitMob Reader reads at most 64 word pieces, which is about the headline and the first lines. It was also taught on headline plus a short teaser, not on whole articles. On our test set, 1,115 of 1,600 items were already cut off at that limit.

Real articles are much longer, so the reader was missing most of the story. Making the limit bigger on these short test items did not help much, because the items were short to begin with.

Reading the whole article helps the BitMob Reader, by about three points

"Right within two guesses" means the correct topic is one of the reader's two best guesses. We scored the reader on 1,600 articles whose topics were labelled by people. Reading the whole article scored 0.825 against 0.794 for headline plus teaser.

Headline only0.804
Headline plus teaser (as built)0.794
Whole article0.825

The gain is 0.031, and the likely range is 0.019 to 0.043. It is real but small. We also found the headline was pushing some body text out of each window; fixing that changed the score by -0.0031, so it did not matter.

A plain keyword model, taught on human labels, did better

We then tried the simplest thing: count words and word pairs, and let a basic statistical model learn from 8,716 articles that people had labelled. It is not a neural network. It takes about 993 microseconds an article.

It might be a trick of the test, so we checked. We removed three whole publishers from training and tested only on them, and we trained on earlier months and tested on later ones.

Knowing when to trust an answer is done by proved rules, not by guessing

If the keyword model only answers when its two best guesses carry at least 70 percent of its confidence, it answers 74% of articles and is right on 96% of those. The rest go on to the BitMob Reader.

Answered when sure: share of articles0.744
Right within two on those0.961
Right within two on the ones held back0.790

The rules that decide what counts as evidence, and what may become training data, are written in Lean 4 and checked by the computer. They say, for example, that Claude agreeing with itself is never evidence, and that an answer from a trained model is never used as a training label. The same rules are copied into Rust, and every build replays all 160 of Lean's cases; one deliberately wrong rule made the build fail.

One topic misses its bar: arts, culture, entertainment and media reaches only 0.77, so topic still counts as provisional.

Speed: about 77 ms an article today, and why Rust alone will not make it a millisecond

The whole tool takes a median 77 ms an article on 8 threads and 183 ms on one. Of that, the BitMob Reader takes 33 ms and the number-and-date solver, mostly the spaCy parser, takes 42 ms. The decider is 0.012 ms.

The Rust decider takes 6 nanoseconds, but nothing calls it yet, and it was never the slow part. The keyword model is about 993 microseconds. A rule-based stand-in for spaCy took 0.3 ms but agreed with spaCy only 0.854 of the time on amounts, below the 0.957 you get by calling every fact stated, so it is not good enough.

Words used on this page

BitMob Reader (v6b-student)
The small neural model, registry code PULSEB, that reads a story and scores story type, topic and more. It runs on a CPU.
Keyword model
Counts words and word pairs and uses a basic statistical model. Not in the registry yet.
Solver
Rules plus the spaCy parser. It finds amounts and dates and checks the text really says them. It learns nothing.
Evidence filter and decider
The Lean-proved rules that say whether a number counts as evidence, and whether to answer or abstain on one article.
Human labels, Claude labels
Human labels come from public datasets labelled by people (MN-DS here). Claude labels were written by Claude Sonnet and Haiku to teach the reader; agreement with them is not accuracy.
Abstain
Decline to answer and send the article somewhere else.
Clef, BitMob classifier
Clef is the GPU model the World engine uses today. The BitMob classifier is an older CPU path. Neither is measured on this page.
