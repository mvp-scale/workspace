How BitMob works: from a news story to every answer the engine needs

BitMob is our small, fast reader. The World engine needs a reader to answer questions about each news story (is it worth reading, what is changing, where, how serious). Today that reader is Clef, a large model that runs on a graphics card (a GPU) and takes several seconds per story. BitMob's job is to give the same answers on an ordinary computer chip (a CPU) in a fraction of a second. This page follows one real story through BitMob, then explains how it was taught and how close it is. Every number is read from a file; the sources are listed at the bottom.

0 · One real story

This is the second story in our answer key: 2,000 news stories that Clef has already answered and that BitMob has never been trained on, so they work as an exam. It was not chosen for how well BitMob did on it. BitMob sees only the headline and the opening of the article.


  


  

    
1 · Words become tokens, tokens become numbers

    
A token is a small piece of a word (often a whole word). The reader's first part, the encoder, splits the story into tokens and turns each token into a list of numbers. Below, each row is one token and each coloured cell is one of its numbers (blue = negative, orange = positive). Hover a cell for its value. The numbers for a token depend on the words around it, so "bill" next to "labour" gets different numbers than "bill" next to "electricity".

2 · The whole story becomes ONE list of numbers

    
The encoder averages all the token rows into a single row. A list of numbers like this is called a vector. This one row is everything the rest of BitMob ever sees of the story. The old BitMob (the 16 Oct version) used a smaller encoder (384 numbers per token) that was frozen, meaning it was never changed by our training. The current one is the e5-base encoder (768 numbers per token), and it is fine-tuned end to end: during training every one of its internal settings is adjusted, not just the answer layer on top. That is why it reads our kind of news better.

3 · One shared reader, eleven groups of answers (one per engine step)

    
From that one row of numbers, a small answer layer called a head works out each kind of answer. A head is the simplest possible layer: for each answer it multiplies the 768 numbers by 768 learned weights, adds them up, and turns the total into a probability (a number between 0 and 1, where 0.9 means "90% sure yes"). BitMob has one head group for each thing the engine needs, all sharing the same encoder. Before, there was one separate small classifier per yes/no question; now one pass answers everything.
